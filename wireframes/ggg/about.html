— From the Bench —

I make furniture
the way the old timers
made it. With less.

The hand-tool tradition in these mountains runs deep — every salvage barn within an hour of here has a Stanley No. 5, a backsaw, a brace and bit, and a homemade dogwood mallet hanging on a peg. The makers who put them there built every piece of furniture in their houses with those eight tools and no fasteners. I run a small bandsaw, a thickness planer, a table saw, and a router table, and everything else still happens at the bench.

The old timers thought power tools were corner-cutting. I think they were three-quarters right. A good bandsaw saves four hours of rip-sawing a 10-foot slab and gives you the same result. A poorly-tuned table saw makes a joint that nobody but you will ever notice is slightly off — until they sit at the table for ten years and the joint opens a hair. Then everybody notices.

So the rule is: machines for the heavy lifting where they don't compromise the result. Hands for everything that holds the piece together.

Method № 01

Source from the land.

Every species in the wood library was felled within walking distance of the shop. Most came from Goldberry Grove. None of it was bought from a wholesale supplier.

Method № 02

Air-dry, never kiln.

Kiln-drying is fast and cheaper. It also locks stresses into the wood that show up two years after delivery. Air-drying takes years and gives you wood that's already finished its movement.

Method № 03

Joinery, then fasteners.

Mortise and tenon, dovetail, wedged through-tenon, slip-cleat. A metal fastener appears when it's actually the right answer — drawer slides on a sewing cabinet, for example — and not before.

Method № 04

Finishes you can repair.

Tung oil and beeswax. Refinishable with a bottle and a soft cloth, by the owner, ten years from now, without sanding back to bare wood.

Method № 05

Provenance, on the underside.

Each piece carries a letterpressed card identifying the tree it came from, when it was planted, when it was felled, and the storm. Signed and numbered.

Method № 06

Lifetime structural guarantee.

If the joinery fails, I'll repair it — for the lifetime of the piece, regardless of who owns it. No fine print, no expiry date.

— A Note on Pacing —
“Twelve pieces a year.
Sometimes thirteen.
Never twenty.”

I get asked once a quarter if I can scale up. The honest answer is that I could do twenty pieces a year if I hired a second bench and let go of the joinery on three of every five pieces. I won't. The work is the work.

If you want a piece, the lead time is twelve to fourteen weeks for the catalog and six to nine months for a commission. The wait is part of what you're buying.
