The land —
and how we came to it.

The twenty acres that became Goldberry Grove were bought in 2024 by Josh and Abigail Dunbar — neither of us from farming families, both of us still holding day jobs while we figure out whether this can work. The property is on a south-facing slope in the Appalachian foothills above the New River Gorge, with a stream that runs year-round, an old hay barn that's now the workshop, and a stand of shagbark hickory and black walnut that were here long before us.

We didn't have a plan, exactly. We had a thesis: that the trees already growing on this hillside knew more about what wanted to live here than any plan we could draw on paper. The first year was mostly mapping — what was where, what produced, what was sick, what could carry more weight. By year two we had a rough food-forest design that worked with the canopy rather than under it.

We pulled the design language from the Savanna Institute's multi-strata work and adapted it for Appalachian hardwood. Upper canopy: hickory + walnut for nuts, mast, and eventually timber. Sub-canopy: pawpaw, persimmon, serviceberry. Shrub: elderberry, hazel, currant. Herbaceous: ramps, medicinal perennials, and shiitake-inoculated hardwood logs in the deep shade. We are two years in. It is starting to function. It is starting to teach us.

No. 01

Read the land first.

The trees on this hillside were here before we were. Our job is to add layers, not replace them. The food forest is designed around what was already growing — not the other way around.

No. 02

Closed-loop fertility.

Leaf litter, on-site compost, and rotational mulching account for nearly all of our soil amendment. Hardwood leaves are an Appalachian gift; we don't import what we already have for free.

No. 03

Small batches, signed.

Nothing leaves the barn that isn't dated, signed, and accounted for. If you have a question about a bottle of syrup you bought in 2024, we can tell you which afternoon it came off the boil.

No. 04

The slowest possible scale.

We have turned down two wholesale offers and one television show. Not out of pride — out of arithmetic. The hillside produces what the hillside produces.

A note from Josh & Abigail

We are not the third generation.
We are the first.

A lot of farm-website "about" pages start with a grandfather who planted a tree fifty years ago. We don't have that story. We have this one: two people in their thirties, both with non-agricultural backgrounds, who bought a piece of West Virginia hillside in 2024 because we wanted to know what it took to actually feed a place.

If you've bought something from us, you've helped pay for the next round of hardwood shiitake logs, the new cover on the syrup-boil shed, and most of the firewood that will heat the farmhouse this winter. Thank you. Truly.

A Brief Timeline

Two years, in four dates.

  • 2023 — Josh & Abigail buy the twenty acres. Both keep day jobs. The food-forest design is sketched against what's already growing.
  • 2024 — First hardwood shiitake logs inoculated. First pawpaws planted. At The Grove Nursery opens, propagating native + grafted stock alongside the farm.
  • 2025 — First shagbark syrup batch. GGG Woodworking starts in the old hay barn, milling from the land.
  • 2026 — Year two. Three small businesses on one hillside.
