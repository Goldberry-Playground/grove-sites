Shagbark Hickory Syrup

$28.00 — 8oz bottle

A single-origin syrup rendered from the loose, peeling bark of Carya ovata — the shagbark hickory native to our hillside. The bark is winter-collected from felled-on-site trees (never from living wood), gently roasted, and slow-reduced with a small percentage of maple sap. Smoky, butterscotch-deep, and unlike anything you've poured before.

A small note: Hickory syrup is a slower, smokier cousin of maple. The first taste reads as caramel; the long finish reads as wood smoke. If you've never tried it, start with a teaspoon over plain yogurt.

The story of
this syrup.

Shagbark hickory is one of the great forgotten ingredients of Appalachian foodways. The native peoples of this region used it for centuries — a winter syrup made from bark scraped after the first hard frost, when the inner cambium has gone sweet. We learned the method from an oral history of foodways in the New River Gorge collected in the 1970s.

Our trees are mature second-growth — most are between fifty and eighty years old, predating us by decades. We don't tap living trees. When a shagbark blows down in a winter storm or has to be taken out for a stand-thinning, we collect the loose plate-bark in February and the wood goes to the workshop at GGG. Nothing wasted.

Each 50-gallon batch yields about sixty 8-ounce bottles. We make four batches a year, all in winter. The syrup is bottled hot, sealed, and rested in the cellar for two weeks before it ships. The flavor deepens visibly between weeks one and three after the boil.
