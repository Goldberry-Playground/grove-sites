What we mean
when we say
“agroforestry.”

We farm twenty acres in the Appalachian foothills above the New River Gorge — and the central word in that sentence is forest. The shagbark hickories, black walnuts, oaks and cherries on this hillside were here before us. Our work is to read what the land already does well and farm with it, not on top of it.

That looks like five layers stacked into one acre: a high canopy of hickory and walnut for nuts and timber; a sub-canopy of pawpaw, persimmon and serviceberry; a shrub layer of elderberry, hazelnut and currant; an herbaceous layer of medicinal and culinary perennials; and shiitake-inoculated hardwood logs in the cool understory shade. (Apprentice readers, see the Savanna Institute on multi-strata food systems — we draw heavily from their work.)

What you find on the shop page is what we render, dry, press, infuse, or pack from that layered system. Quantities are small. Reorders are rare. Each batch is dated, signed, and shipped from a barn that smells of hickory smoke or walnut hull or a slow rendering of pawpaw, depending on the week.

A managed Appalachian food forest doesn't replace conventional rows — it stacks productivity vertically. Sunlight reaches all five layers because each is tuned to a different light envelope. Here's what each layer gives us at Goldberry.

A Letter from the Hollow

“The hickory nuts came in three weeks late this year. The whole syrup-boil rotation moved with them.”

The advantage of working a small Appalachian farm is that we can change our minds in twenty-four hours. The disadvantage is that everything depends on whether we read the weather right. This year, the shagbark crop came in by late October — three weeks behind last year, and the canopy-down rotation (nuts first, syrup-boil second, mushroom flush third) had to compress.

We shifted the syrup boil into November, kept the shiitake logs under shade-cloth longer than planned, and the bees — who follow neither calendar nor opinion — did exactly what they wanted.
