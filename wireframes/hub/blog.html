Notes from
an Appalachian village.

The shops sell things. The journal is the rest of it — community, books, mountain living, ecology, resilience that isn't about prepping, and the longer conversations behind why we ended up here. Some of it is about farming. Most of it isn't.

Featured Essay · Community · May 14, 2026 · 9-min read · By Josh Dunbar

We get asked, more than any other question, why we chose West Virginia. The honest answer is layered — cheap land is in it, the New River Gorge being one of the most beautiful places in the country is in it, the proximity to a dozen towns whose names we still mispronounce is in it — but underneath all of that is the part that's harder to put into a real-estate brochure. We chose West Virginia because we wanted to live somewhere that hadn't already decided what it was going to be.

The Appalachian narrative most of America carries around is a flattened one. You know it. We knew it before we moved here. The version of these mountains that exists in the national imagination is about absence — coal that left, jobs that left, young people that left, opioids that came in to fill the silence. That story is real. It's also one frame, applied selectively to a place that contains a hundred other frames.

The frame nobody runs on the evening news.

The frame we live in is a county where the agricultural extension office runs a free shiitake-cultivation workshop in March and seventy people show up. Where the regional food hub is run by a woman who used to manage a hedge fund and quit to come home to West Virginia. Where the local hardware store is a co-op that has been in continuous operation since 1923, where you can still buy a single bolt by weight, and where the man who runs it has, in the past month, given us a sermon on the importance of three-quarter-inch over half-inch plumbing in cold-weather barns.

“Resilience, the way we mean it, isn't a survivalist disposition. It's a community one. We get through winter because there are six people we could call at midnight, and most of them would come.”

If you grew up reading about Appalachia from the outside, this will sound like a counter-narrative. To people who've lived here for any length of time, it's just a Tuesday. The story of these mountains that runs in The New York Times is true, partial, and decades-old. The story of these mountains that the people in them tell each other on porches in the evenings has a wider vocabulary.

The reading list that brought us here.

We read seventy books before we moved. Some of them were about farming, but the ones that actually moved the needle weren't. Wendell Berry on community. Silas House on place. Elena Passarello on attention. Robert Macfarlane on language and land. The Berry essay collection The Art of the Commonplace sat on the kitchen table for three years before we worked up the courage to put an offer in on the twenty acres. We still re-read it once a year.

The journal here is going to talk about books. It's going to talk about music — there's a string-band scene within thirty miles of us that nobody outside Appalachia is paying attention to, and it should be. It's going to talk about the food-hub economy and the regional climate adaptation work and the new hardwood-mast research from the savanna institute. It's going to talk about agroforestry, because that's our day job. But mostly it's going to talk about the village — what holds a place together when the national story says it shouldn't be held together at all.

If that sounds like the kind of thing you want to read, you're already on the email list. (You're not, actually. You're on the wireframe. But the email list will exist, once we build it.) If it sounds like the kind of thing you want to write with us, we have a couple of guest-essay slots open every quarter. Reach out.
